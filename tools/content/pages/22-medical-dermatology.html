Moles: when to get one checked

Most moles are harmless, but melanoma, a serious skin cancer, can start in a mole or look like a new one. The ABCDE signs help: Asymmetry, an irregular Border, uneven Colour, a Diameter usually larger than 6 mm, and Evolving, meaning any change in size, shape or colour. A mole that itches, bleeds or looks different from your other moles should also be examined. In darker skin, skin cancer is often found late, and it can appear on the palms, soles, nails or inside the mouth, or as a sore that does not heal.

When a mole looks unusual, the right treatment is surgical removal with a margin of normal skin, followed by laboratory examination. Lasers, cautery and freezing are not used for these moles, because they leave no tissue to examine, and Indian guidelines on radiofrequency surgery note that heat damage can interfere with laboratory testing. For raised moles that are clearly harmless, removal for comfort or appearance is possible, but it can leave a mark, and moles removed by shaving or radiofrequency are more likely to grow back than those cut out completely.

Warts: treatment and recurrence

Warts are spread by touch and by sharing towels or footwear. Many clear without treatment, so for a small wart that does not bother you, waiting is reasonable. When treatment is wanted, options include salicylic acid, freezing, radiofrequency (electrosurgery) and scraping. After electrosurgery, healing usually takes two weeks or longer, around one in five warts comes back within a few months, and a small scar is possible. Warts on the soles may need several sessions. Genital warts are different: they need assessment at a sexual health clinic, and pharmacy wart treatments should not be used on them.

Skin tags: safe removal

Skin tags are not dangerous and do not turn into cancer, so removal is a personal choice. They can be removed with heat (radiofrequency or cautery), by freezing or by snipping them off with a sterile blade or scissors. Larger tags or those on the eyelids need extra care. Tying them off with thread, cutting them at home or using removal creams risks infection, bleeding and scars. A doctor also makes sure that what looks like a skin tag is not another kind of growth.

Common skin problems we see

Alongside growths, many people come with long-term skin problems. Eczema cannot be cured, but treatment can control itching and flares. Psoriasis is not contagious and is a long-term condition managed with medical treatment. Fungal infections such as ringworm need an antifungal used every day for up to four weeks, even after the rash fades. In India, steroid creams sold without prescription, often mixed with antifungals, are a major cause of stubborn, widespread ringworm, so we avoid them and help you stop them safely. If your condition needs specialist care, we will tell you and refer you.

Some rashes need urgent care rather than a clinic appointment: a rash with fever, a rash that does not fade when a glass is pressed on it, blistering, a rash that spreads quickly or one that affects the eyes, mouth or genitals. A growth that bleeds, crusts or has not healed for more than four weeks should also be seen promptly.

Aftercare after removal

  • Keep the dressing on for the time advised, then clean the area gently and apply the recommended ointment.
  • Let the scab fall off on its own; picking it increases the risk of a mark.
  • Avoid swimming pools, saunas and heavy sweating until the skin has healed.
  • Use sunscreen on healed skin for a few months to help pink or dark marks settle.
  • Contact the clinic if you notice increasing redness, swelling, pus, fever or bleeding that does not stop with pressure.
