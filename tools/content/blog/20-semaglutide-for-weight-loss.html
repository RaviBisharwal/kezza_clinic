The short answer

Semaglutide is a prescription medicine that helps adults with obesity lose weight by copying GLP-1, a natural gut hormone that controls appetite and fullness. For weight loss it is usually given as a once-weekly injection under the skin, the dose is increased step by step, and it works best alongside changes to eating and activity. It is not suitable for everyone, stomach side effects are common, and it should only be used under a doctor's supervision.

It was first approved to treat type 2 diabetes. A higher-dose version is used for long-term weight management in adults with obesity, or with overweight and a weight-related health problem. Semaglutide is the active ingredient in brands such as Ozempic®, which is approved for type 2 diabetes, and Wegovy®, which is approved for weight management.

How semaglutide works

Semaglutide belongs to a group of medicines called GLP-1 receptor agonists. It acts like the hormone your gut releases after a meal, but it lasts much longer in the body, which is why one injection a week is enough.

  • Appetite: it acts on the parts of the brain that control hunger and fullness, so many people feel less hungry.
  • Slower stomach emptying: food stays in the stomach longer, so you feel full sooner and for longer after eating.
  • Blood sugar: it helps the body release insulin when blood sugar is high, which is why it is also used for type 2 diabetes.
  • Not a fat burner: it does not melt fat directly. Weight comes down because you eat less, so what you eat still matters.

How much weight can you lose?

Key evidence comes from the STEP 1 trial, published in the New England Journal of Medicine. Adults without diabetes who had obesity (a BMI of 30 or more), or overweight (a BMI of 27 or more) with a weight-related health problem, used weekly semaglutide or a placebo for 68 weeks, and both groups also had support to change their diet and activity.

These are averages. Some people lose much more and some lose much less, and weight comes off gradually over months. People with type 2 diabetes tend to lose less: in the STEP 2 trial, the same weekly dose led to an average loss of about 10% of body weight over 68 weeks, compared with about 3% on placebo. The NHS notes that losing even 5 to 10% of your body weight can improve your health.

Benefits beyond the scale

In STEP 1, waist size, blood pressure and blood sugar also improved more with semaglutide than with placebo. The larger SELECT trial followed more than 17,000 adults aged 45 and over who already had heart or blood-vessel disease, such as a previous heart attack or stroke, and had overweight or obesity but not diabetes. Over about three years, weekly semaglutide lowered the risk of a heart attack, a stroke or death from heart or blood-vessel disease by 20% compared with placebo: 6.5% of people on semaglutide had one of these events, against 8.0% on placebo.

These benefits depend on staying on treatment. In the STEP 1 follow-up, the improvements in blood pressure and blood sugar largely faded within a year after people stopped.

Who may be suitable

Doctors usually consider a weight-loss medicine such as semaglutide for adults who:

  • have obesity, or have overweight together with a weight-related health problem such as type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure or high cholesterol;
  • have tried changing their eating and activity, but have not been able to lose enough weight or keep it off; and
  • are ready to continue those lifestyle changes alongside the medicine, with regular follow-up.

For Asian Indians, health risks start at a lower weight. A 2025 consensus of Indian experts treats a body mass index (BMI) above 23 as obesity, and uses waist size and the effect of weight on health to judge how serious it is, so your doctor looks at your waist and your overall health, not just the number on the scale. A lower cut-off does not by itself mean you need a medicine: your doctor decides based on your BMI, waist, health and the medicine's approved use. Semaglutide is not meant for losing a few kilos before an event. This guide is about adults; for anyone under 18, treatment is a decision for a specialist.

Who should not use it

  • People with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid cancer, or a condition called multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2 (MEN 2). Semaglutide caused thyroid tumours in laboratory animals; it is not known whether it does this in people, but the US prescribing information carries a boxed warning about it.
  • Women who are pregnant or breastfeeding. The NHS advises stopping semaglutide at least 2 months before trying for a baby, and using contraception while taking it if pregnancy is possible.
  • People who have had an allergic reaction to semaglutide or any of its ingredients.

It may not be suitable if you have ever had an inflamed pancreas (pancreatitis) or an allergic reaction to another GLP-1 medicine. It is not a treatment for type 1 diabetes and does not replace insulin. Also tell your doctor before starting if you have diabetic eye disease, gallbladder or kidney disease, severe stomach or bowel problems such as slow stomach emptying (gastroparesis), if you take insulin or sulphonylurea tablets for diabetes (the risk of low blood sugar is higher), or if you have surgery planned.

Doses and how it is taken

Semaglutide is injected just under the skin of the abdomen, thigh or upper arm once a week, on the same day each week, changing the spot each time. Treatment starts at a low dose that is raised every four weeks, so the body can adjust and stomach side effects are easier to manage. In the STEP 1 trial, the dose was increased like this:

The diabetes version starts with the same low doses but has a lower top dose. Your doctor decides how fast to increase the dose, may keep you on a lower step if side effects are troublesome, and will tell you what to do if you miss a dose. Never change the dose yourself, never share a pen or needle, and store the medicine exactly as instructed.

Side effects

The NHS lists feeling or being sick, constipation, diarrhoea, tiredness, hair loss and itchy or red skin where you inject among the common side effects. They are usually worst when starting or after a dose increase, and often ease with time. Eating smaller meals, stopping when you feel full, avoiding greasy or fried food and drinking enough water can help.

Get medical help urgently if you have:

  • severe stomach pain that may spread to your back, with or without vomiting (possible pancreatitis);
  • pain in the upper stomach, fever, yellowing of the skin or eyes, or pale stools (possible gallbladder problems);
  • vomiting or diarrhoea so severe that you cannot keep fluids down, which can lead to dehydration and kidney problems;
  • swelling of the face or throat, a rash or difficulty breathing (an allergic reaction);
  • sudden loss of vision, usually in one eye, or any other change in your vision;
  • signs of low blood sugar, such as sweating, shaking or a fast heartbeat, if you also take diabetes medicines;
  • a lump or swelling in the neck, hoarseness, trouble swallowing or shortness of breath.

Protecting your muscles while you lose weight

As with any large weight loss, part of the weight lost can be muscle rather than fat. Eating enough protein, spreading it across your meals and doing strength exercises two or more times a week can help protect muscle. Your doctor or a dietitian can set targets that suit you.

What happens when you stop

Semaglutide controls appetite only while you are taking it. In a follow-up of the STEP 1 trial, people who stopped both semaglutide and the trial's lifestyle programme regained, on average, about two-thirds of the weight they had lost within one year. That is why treatment is planned as part of a long-term approach: habits around food, activity and sleep that you can keep, regular reviews, and a clear plan with your doctor about how long to continue and how to stop. If you have diabetes, do not stop suddenly without medical advice.

Semaglutide in India: prescriptions and generics

Semaglutide tablets have been available in India for type 2 diabetes since 2022, and the weekly injections for weight management and for diabetes were launched here in 2025. After the patent expired in March 2026, many Indian companies began selling semaglutide under their own names. Doctors have warned that wider availability should not lead to self-medication.

It remains a prescription-only medicine. The Indian government has said these medicines are approved on the condition that they are prescribed by specialists, mainly endocrinologists or internal medicine specialists (and other specialists, such as cardiologists, for some approved uses), and it has stepped up checks on unauthorised sales. Use semaglutide only after a qualified doctor has assessed you, and get it from a licensed pharmacy. Avoid websites, gyms or sellers that do not ask for a prescription, unbranded vials and mixed (compounded) versions. The US Food and Drug Administration warns that unapproved and counterfeit versions can contain the wrong ingredient, too much or too little of it, or none at all, and it has received reports of side effects, some needing hospital care, that may have been caused by dosing mistakes with compounded semaglutide.

Semaglutide and tirzepatide

Tirzepatide is a related weekly injection that acts on two gut hormones, GIP and GLP-1, rather than one. In its main trial, SURMOUNT-1, adults with obesity, or overweight with a weight-related health problem, lost on average 15% to 21% of their body weight at 72 weeks, depending on the dose, compared with about 3% on placebo. Side effects are similar, mainly stomach-related. Which medicine is right for you, if any, depends on your health, your other medicines and how you respond, and is a decision to make with your doctor.
