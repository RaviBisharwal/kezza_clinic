About Glutathione

What is glutathione?

Glutathione is a small molecule made in almost every cell of the body from three amino acids. It is one of the body's main antioxidants, helping to neutralise free radicals and supporting the liver's normal detoxification work.

Its link with skin comes from laboratory research: glutathione appears to reduce the activity of tyrosinase, an enzyme needed to make melanin, and to shift melanin production from the darker type (eumelanin) towards a lighter type (pheomelanin). Whether that translates into a visible, lasting change in people is much less certain.

Glutathione is available as tablets, creams and injections or intravenous (IV) infusions. In clinics it is usually given as a course of sessions. Ask your doctor which route and product your plan uses — the evidence and the risks differ by route, as the table below shows.

At Kezza we see glutathione as an optional add-on, not a solution for pigmentation. Problems such as melasma, tan and dark spots respond better to sun protection and treatments with stronger evidence, such as medical peels, Q-switch laser and prescription creams — see our pigmentation treatment page.

The Evidence

What does the research say?

A widely cited review in the Indian Journal of Dermatology, Venereology and Leprology (Sonthalia, Daulatabad and Sarkar, 2016) looked at the evidence in detail. Its main findings:

  • Creams and tablets: a few small, short trials found a modest lightening effect in some people, with few side effects. How long any effect lasts, and what dose works, is not known.
  • Injections and IV drips: there were no good-quality trials showing that intravenous glutathione lightens skin, and the authors advised dermatologists not to use it for skin lightening until better evidence exists.
  • Effects fade: any lightening appears to be temporary once glutathione is stopped.

Regulators have also raised concerns. The US Food and Drug Administration states that it has not approved any injectable product for whitening or lightening skin, and has warned that such products may be unsafe, contaminated or counterfeit. The Philippines FDA has warned against off-label intravenous glutathione for skin lightening after reports of side effects.

That is why your doctor will talk you through the evidence, the risks and the alternatives before you decide — and why we never promise a change in skin colour.

Suitability

Who should — and should not — have glutathione?

You may consider it if

  • You are a healthy adult and understand the evidence is limited
  • You want a doctor-supervised course rather than unregulated products
  • You already use sunscreen daily and have tried proven treatments
  • You accept that results vary and may fade after stopping

Not suitable, or check with your doctor, if

  • You are pregnant, trying to conceive or breastfeeding
  • You are under 18
  • You have kidney, liver or thyroid disease, or asthma
  • You are having cancer treatment — glutathione may interfere with some medicines
  • You have had a serious allergic reaction to any medicine or injection

Never buy injectable glutathione online or have it given outside a medical setting. Counterfeit and contaminated products have caused serious harm.

Safety

Side effects and safety

Possible and usually mild

  • Pain, bruising or swelling where an injection or drip is given
  • Nausea, stomach discomfort or a headache
  • Skin rash or itching

Rare but serious

  • Severe allergic reactions, including breathing difficulty
  • Serious skin reactions with blistering or peeling
  • Infection from unsafe injection practices
  • Harm from counterfeit or contaminated products
  • Effects on the kidneys, liver or thyroid have been raised by regulators; long-term safety is not known

Seek urgent medical help if you develop breathing difficulty, swelling of the face or lips, a spreading rash, blistering skin or fever after a session.

Frequently asked questions

We do not offer glutathione as a fairness treatment. Small studies of tablets and creams have shown a modest lightening effect in some people, there is no good evidence that injections or drips work, and any effect tends to fade after stopping.

Tablets and creams are usually well tolerated. Injections and drips carry more risk, including rare but serious allergic and skin reactions, and danger from unregulated products. That is why it is given only after a medical assessment and in the clinic, never at home or from online products.

The usual course at Kezza is 10 sessions. Your doctor reviews the result at the end; if there is no benefit, there is no reason to continue.
