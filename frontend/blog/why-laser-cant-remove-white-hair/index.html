White Hair Reduction

Why Laser Can't Remove White Hair, and What Does

If laser sessions cleared your dark hair but left the white ones behind, the machine isn't faulty. It's how laser hair reduction works.

By the Kezza Hair & Skin Clinic team Updated 4 September 2026 4 min read

Key takeaways

  • Hair-reduction lasers target melanin, the pigment in dark hair. White and grey hair has little or no melanin, so it doesn't absorb enough laser energy to be disabled.
  • Blonde and red hair also respond poorly, for the same reason.
  • Electrolysis works on any hair colour because it treats each follicle directly with a fine probe and a small current. The US FDA describes it as a permanent clinical method.
  • Electrolysis needs several sessions. At Kezza most patients need 6 to 12, spaced 4 to 8 weeks apart, depending on the area.

The short answer

Laser hair reduction works by heating the pigment (melanin) in dark hair. White and grey hairs have little or no melanin, so the laser has nothing to target and the follicle is not damaged. Blonde and red hair respond poorly for the same reason. The method that does work on white and grey hair is electrolysis, which treats each follicle directly with a very fine probe and a small electrical current, whatever the hair colour.

How laser hair reduction works

Laser hair reduction is based on a principle called selective photothermolysis: a specific wavelength of light is absorbed by a specific target, heating it while leaving the surrounding skin largely unharmed. For hair reduction, the target is melanin in the hair shaft and follicle. The absorbed energy turns into heat and damages the follicle's ability to grow hair.

That is why laser works best when there is a clear contrast between dark hair and lighter skin, and why most people need about 4 to 8 sessions. In the US, the FDA allows lasers to claim "permanent hair reduction", meaning a long-term, stable reduction in the number of hairs that regrow, rather than removal of every hair.

Why white and grey hairs don't respond

As hair turns grey and then white, the follicle stops producing melanin. Without pigment, the laser's light passes through the hair instead of being absorbed, so the follicle never heats up enough to be damaged. It does not matter which laser is used: diode, Nd:YAG, alexandrite and IPL devices all rely on melanin.

Why not just turn up the power? With no pigment in the hair, the extra energy has nowhere useful to go. Higher settings mainly increase the risk of burns, blisters and pigment changes in the skin, which is a particular concern in darker Indian skin tones.

What about blonde, red or fine hair?

Mayo Clinic notes that blonde, white and grey hair have less pigment, so laser is less effective, and that red hair contains a different type of melanin that does not absorb enough laser energy. Very fine, light hair often responds poorly too. For these hair types, electrolysis is usually the more reliable option.

Electrolysis: the option that works on white hair

Electrolysis treats hairs one at a time. A very fine, sterile probe is slipped into the natural opening of the hair follicle, and a small, controlled current is delivered to the root. This damages the follicle's growth centre so it cannot produce a new hair. Because it does not depend on pigment, it works on white, grey, blonde and red hair, and on any skin tone.

The US FDA describes electrolysis as a permanent clinical method, since it destroys the hair follicle. It still takes several sessions, because hairs grow in cycles: at any one visit, only some follicles are in the active growth phase when treatment is most effective, and new hairs surface over the following weeks.

What to expect from electrolysis

  • Before: a numbing cream is usually applied to the area beforehand.
  • During: most people feel warmth or a brief pinch with each hair.
  • After: mild redness or small bumps usually settle within hours to a day. Avoid scrubbing, heavy makeup and sun on the area for a day or so, as your doctor advises.
  • Safety: the FDA notes that risks include infection from an unsterile needle and scarring from improper technique, which is why electrolysis should be done with sterile, single-use probes by trained staff.

FAQs

Frequently Asked Questions

Can laser remove grey hair at all?
Not reliably. Lasers need pigment in the hair to absorb their energy. Grey hairs that still have a little pigment may respond slightly, but fully white hairs do not. Increasing the laser's power does not solve this; it only raises the risk of burns and pigment changes in the skin.
Is electrolysis permanent?
Electrolysis is considered a permanent hair reduction and elimination method because it destroys the growth centre of each treated follicle; the US FDA describes it this way. Several sessions are needed because hairs grow in cycles and not every follicle is active at the same time.
How many electrolysis sessions will I need?
At Kezza, most patients need 6 to 12 sessions spaced 4 to 8 weeks apart. Small areas such as the upper lip may clear in fewer sessions, while larger areas such as the beard line need more.
Is electrolysis safe for Indian skin?
Yes, when done correctly. Because electrolysis treats the follicle directly rather than relying on pigment, skin tone is not the limiting factor it is with lasers. Temporary redness is common. Poor technique or unsterile equipment can cause infection or scarring, so choose a medical setting.
Can I combine laser and electrolysis?
Yes. Many people use laser for dark hairs, where it works well and covers large areas quickly, and electrolysis for the white, grey or blonde hairs that laser leaves behind.
