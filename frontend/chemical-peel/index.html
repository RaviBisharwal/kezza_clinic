The Treatment

What is a chemical peel?

Your skin sheds dead cells all the time, but sun, pollution, oil and age slow this down, leaving skin dull, rough or blotchy and pores congested. A chemical peel speeds up the process in a controlled way: the acid loosens the bonds between dead cells so they lift away over the next few days, and the skin renews itself from below.

Peels are grouped by how deep they work. Superficial peels, such as glycolic, salicylic, lactic and mandelic acid, act on the outermost layer and have little downtime — they are what most people need for glow, acne and tan. Medium-depth peels go further, cause more peeling and are planned by a doctor for specific concerns; see medical peels. Deep peels are rarely used in Indian skin.

Results build over a course. One peel can make skin look brighter within a week; for acne, texture and pigment, a course of about 3 sessions a few weeks apart works better, together with good daily skin care and sunscreen.

Indian skin can darken after irritation (post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation), so the peel strength, time on the skin and preparation matter. This is why peels at Kezza are chosen and supervised by doctors rather than applied as a standard salon treatment.

Peel Types

Common superficial peels

These are the acids doctors most often use for superficial peels. Your doctor chooses the one that suits your skin.

Salicylic acid

Oil-soluble, so it gets into pores — a common choice for oily, acne-prone skin and blackheads.

Glycolic acid

A small-molecule fruit acid for dullness, rough texture and early fine lines.

Lactic acid

A gentler acid that also hydrates, often used for dry or sensitive skin.

Mandelic acid

A larger molecule that penetrates slowly, often chosen for sensitive or darker skin with acne or pigment.

Suitability

Is a chemical peel right for you?

A peel may help if you have

  • Dull, tired-looking skin or a rough texture
  • Blackheads, whiteheads or mild, active acne
  • Tan, uneven tone or light marks after acne
  • An event coming up — with a peel planned at least a week before

Tell your doctor first if

  • You take isotretinoin, or stopped it recently
  • You use retinoids, scrubs or other exfoliating products
  • You have cold sores, eczema, rosacea or an active skin infection
  • You have recently waxed, threaded or had laser on the area
  • You are pregnant or breastfeeding — some peels are avoided

Deep pigmentation such as melasma usually needs a medical peel or laser plan rather than a superficial peel alone — your doctor will tell you.

Step by Step

What happens during a chemical peel

  1. 01Skin check

    Choosing the peel

    Your doctor looks at your skin type, concern and current products, and chooses the peel and strength.

  2. 02Preparation

    1–4 weeks before

    Daily sunscreen and sometimes a prescribed cream to prepare the skin; retinoids are paused a few days before.

  3. 03Cleanse

    On the day

    The skin is cleansed and degreased so the peel spreads evenly.

  4. 04Peel

    A few timed minutes

    The peel is applied and timed carefully. Tingling or mild warmth is normal.

  5. 05Soothe

    Neutralise and protect

    The peel is neutralised or removed, then a soothing cream and sunscreen are applied.

  6. 06Course

    Repeat in a few weeks

    Most people have 3 sessions a few weeks apart, then maintenance if needed.

Safety

Side effects and safety

Common and temporary

  • Stinging or warmth during the peel
  • Redness and tightness for a few hours
  • Light flaking for a few days

Less common

  • Darker patches (post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation), more likely after sun exposure
  • Irritation or an allergic reaction to the peel
  • A cold sore flare in people who get them
  • Rarely, blistering or scarring — more of a risk with stronger peels

Contact the clinic if your skin blisters, oozes, becomes very painful or develops dark patches after a peel.
