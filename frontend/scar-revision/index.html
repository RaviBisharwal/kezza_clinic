The Treatment

What is scar revision?

A scar is the skin's natural way of repairing a wound. Most fade with time, but some stay wide, raised, pitted, discoloured or tight, especially on the face or over joints. Scar revision means treating a scar so that it blends better with the skin around it.

The approach depends on the scar. A wide or stretched scar can be cut out and the skin closed again in layers, leaving a finer line. A scar that runs against the natural skin creases can be re-angled with a Z-plasty or W-plasty, which also releases tight scars. Depressed scars that are tethered to deeper tissue, such as some acne scars, can be released with subcision. Raised scars are often treated with steroid injections, sometimes alongside surgery.

Timing matters. Scars keep changing for 12 to 18 months, so doctors usually wait until a scar has matured before revising it, unless there is a reason to act sooner. After revision, the new scar goes through the same maturing process, which is why the final result takes months to judge.

For acne scars and uneven texture, treatments that rebuild collagen — such as microneedling — are often combined with scar revision for the best overall result.

Scars We Assess

Types of scars and how they are treated

Your doctor examines the scar before suggesting a technique.

Wide or stretched scars

Often after injuries or surgery. They can be excised and closed in layers to leave a finer line.

Scars against skin creases

Re-angled with a Z-plasty or W-plasty so they follow natural lines and are less noticeable.

Pitted and acne scars

Tethered, rolling scars can be released with subcision and combined with collagen-building treatments.

Raised (hypertrophic) scars

Raised but within the original wound; often flattened with steroid injections and silicone.

Keloids

Grow beyond the original wound and can come back after surgery, so they are treated with a combined plan.

Tight scars

Scars that pull, for example after burns or over joints, can be released to improve movement.

Suitability

Is scar revision right for you?

Scar revision may help if

  • Your scar is at least several months old and has stopped changing
  • It is wide, raised, pitted, discoloured or pulls on the skin
  • It bothers you cosmetically or limits movement
  • You are in good general health and understand that the aim is improvement, not removal

Tell your doctor if

  • You or your family tend to form keloids
  • You have active acne, a skin infection or eczema near the scar
  • You smoke, have diabetes, or take blood thinners
  • You are pregnant or breastfeeding
  • The scar is changing, bleeding or itching a lot — it needs to be checked first

Scars from self-injury can be treated too. You will be treated with care and without judgement; if you are struggling right now, please talk to a doctor or someone you trust first.

  5. 05Scar care

    Weeks to months

    Silicone gel or sheets, gentle massage when advised, and daily sunscreen help the new scar settle.

  6. 06Review

    Next steps

    Progress is reviewed and, if planned, the next session is scheduled once the skin has healed.

Healing

What to expect after scar revision

  1. Week 1–2

    Stitches out

    Mild swelling and redness; stitches removed in about 5 to 14 days depending on the site.

  2. Months 1–3

    Pink and firm

    The new scar is often pink and slightly firm — this is normal.

  3. Months 3–6

    Softening

    Colour and thickness improve; further sessions may be planned.

  4. Months 12–18

    Mature scar

    The scar becomes flatter and paler; this is when the final result is judged.

Safety

Risks of scar revision

Common and usually short-lived

  • Swelling, redness and bruising around the treated area
  • Tenderness or tightness while the wound heals
  • A pink new scar that takes months to fade

Less common

  • Infection, bleeding or the wound opening
  • A new scar that is wider, raised or more noticeable than hoped
  • Return of a keloid after treatment
  • Lighter or darker skin colour around the scar
  • Numbness near the treated area

Contact the clinic if the wound becomes increasingly red, hot, swollen or painful, discharges pus, or you develop a fever.
